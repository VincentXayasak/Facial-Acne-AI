What might be going on

It appears that your acne is related to several factors, including hormonal changes and genetics. Research suggests that puberty and androgens can trigger abnormal differentiation of sebaceous progenitor cells, leading to comedones (blackheads or whiteheads), which are the primary lesions in acne [2]. This process is initiated by hormones and is unlikely to be triggered by Cutibacterium acnes, a bacterium often associated with acne. In fact, recent evidence suggests that C. acnes might not affect sebaceous differentiation at all [2].

Another important factor contributing to your acne could be the presence of Cutibacterium acnes itself. This bacterium can produce toxins and lipases that break down sebum into glycerol and free fatty acids, which in turn stimulate hair follicles and lead to acne vulgaris [1]. However, it's worth noting that not all individuals develop acne vulgaris when exposed to C. acnes, as people have different immune responses to pathogens.

What studies suggest

Studies also indicate that the innate immune system plays a vital role in the development of acne. Cutibacterium acnes can trigger an inflammatory response through TLRs ( Toll-like receptors), which can lead to the production of inflammatory factors such as interleukin (IL)-1β [4]. This inflammation can be further exacerbated by the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, a complex that plays essential roles in acne vulgaris [5].

Researchers have also identified several antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) that may have a protective role in infection and could potentially help treat acne. These AMPs include IL-37, hBD-2, RNase 7, and Psoriasin, which can restrict the reproduction of Cutibacterium acnes [4]. Additionally, all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) has been found to negatively affect the inflammatory response induced by C. acnes, suggesting that it may be a useful therapeutic target for acne treatment.

What studies suggest

Studies indicate that diet can play a crucial role in managing acne symptoms. A low-glycemic-load diet has been shown to improve symptoms in acne vulgaris patients, suggesting that dietary changes may be beneficial for acne management [1]. In contrast, the research on topical treatments is more focused on their tolerability and user acceptance. The primary treatment in ICD (irritant contact dermatitis) is avoidance of the offending irritant(s), skin protection from both in the workplace and at home, and use of topical therapy, particularly moisturizing creams [2]. These moisturizers can improve dry skin symptoms, maintain healthy skin, prevent the absorption of exogenous substances, and improve skin barrier recovery.

Research also suggests that acidity itself may play a crucial role in restoring skin barrier function and preventing eczema. Vinegar has been traditionally used in Korean folk medicine to treat various dermatological conditions, including eczema and tinea pedis. In one study, acidic creams were found to have significantly lower eczema scores compared to neutral cream-treated groups [3]. Furthermore, the participants who bathed in artificial acid water showed improved skin barrier function and reduced inflammation.

In terms of specific treatments for acne, a recent study compared two different routines: a novel routine and a benzoyl peroxide (BPO) routine. The results showed that both routines had similar efficacy in reducing objective acne lesion reduction at 12 weeks, but the novel routine was rated higher by user report across multiple domains from direct product preferences to skin appearance and skin feel [4]. Additionally, the novel routine demonstrated less severe redness, itching, and burning with the greatest differences seen at Week 4.
